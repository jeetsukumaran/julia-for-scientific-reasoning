Processing data for plotting across the antimeridian with vector data

Author

Jeet Sukumaran

  1. Explain why antimeridian artifacts arise from coordinate wrap-around in raw longitude data.
  2. Normalize longitude coordinates to a target central meridian using a modular remapping function.
  3. Insert NaN breaks into coordinate arrays to prevent false line segments across antimeridian crossings.
  4. Compute the exact latitude of an antimeridian crossing and split a linestring into two correctly-terminated segments.

The antimeridian artifacts that arise when centering a projection on \(180°\) are a symptom of a more general problem: longitude values in the standard \([-180°, 180°]\) range are not continuous with respect to an arbitrary map center \(\lambda_0\). A segment whose two endpoints have longitudes of \(170°\) and \(-170°\) spans only \(20°\) of arc in reality, but their coordinate difference is \(340°\) — and the renderer, knowing nothing about spherical topology, draws a line spanning nearly the full width of the plot. The same problem arises whenever your data straddle the longitude that lies \(180°\) from \(\lambda_0\): centering on \(-100°\) (central North America) causes artifacts near \(80°\,\text{E}\), for instance.

Fixing the problem requires two distinct operations, depending on the geometry type:

  1. Longitude normalization — remapping each coordinate so that it falls within \([\lambda_0 - 180°,\ \lambda_0 + 180°]\). This alone resolves artifacts in scattered point data and in linestrings whose individual vertices do not cross the boundary.
  2. Segment breaking or splitting — detecting segments that still cross the boundary after normalization and either inserting a NaN break (suppressing the crossing segment) or computing the exact crossing latitude and splitting the geometry into two correctly-terminated pieces.

1 Longitude normalization

The standard longitude domain \([-180°, 180°]\) is just one period of a circular quantity. For a map centered on \(\lambda_0\), the appropriate domain is \([\lambda_0 - 180°,\ \lambda_0 + 180°]\). Remapping a longitude \(\lambda\) into this domain is a single modular arithmetic operation:

\[ \lambda' = \bigl((\lambda - \lambda_0 + 180) \bmod 360\bigr) - 180 + \lambda_0 \]

"""
    normalize_lon(lon, lon_0=0.0)

Remap longitude `lon` (degrees) into the half-open interval
`[lon_0 - 180, lon_0 + 180)`, preserving its position on the circle.
"""
1normalize_lon(lon, lon_0=0.0) = mod(lon - lon_0 + 180.0, 360.0) - 180.0 + lon_0
1
mod(x, 360) returns a value in \([0°, 360°)\); subtracting \(180°\) and re-centering on lon_0 shifts this into \([\lambda_0 - 180°, \lambda_0 + 180°)\).

2 Inserting NaN breaks for crossing linestrings

Point normalization alone is insufficient for linestring data: after normalization, a segment that genuinely crosses the antimeridian will still produce a nearly-\(360°\)-wide artifact, because its two endpoints end up near opposite edges of the \([\lambda_0 - 180°, \lambda_0 + 180°]\) window. The simplest fix is to detect such segments and insert a NaN coordinate pair, which Makie.lines! interprets as a break in the polyline — suppressing the offending segment entirely.

"""
    antimeridian_breaks(lons, lats; lon_0=0.0)

Normalize `lons` to `[lon_0-180, lon_0+180)` and insert `NaN` sentinel
values before any segment whose normalized endpoint gap exceeds 180°.
Returns `(lons_out, lats_out)` suitable for direct use with `lines!`.
"""
function antimeridian_breaks(lons, lats; lon_0=0.0)
1    lons_n  = normalize_lon.(lons, lon_0)
    lons_out = Float64[]
    lats_out = Float64[]
    for i in eachindex(lons_n)
        push!(lons_out, lons_n[i])
        push!(lats_out, lats[i])
2        if i < lastindex(lons_n) && abs(lons_n[i+1] - lons_n[i]) > 180.0
3            push!(lons_out, NaN)
            push!(lats_out, NaN)
        end
    end
    return lons_out, lats_out
end

2

3

TipNaN breaks vs. exact splitting

Inserting NaN breaks removes the crossing segment, leaving a visible gap in the polyline at the antimeridian. For most visualization purposes — species occurrence tracks, migration routes, ship or aircraft paths — this gap is acceptable and clearly communicates the map edge. For contexts where the continuity of the line matters visually (e.g., a contour line or the boundary of a species range polygon), you should instead split the geometry at the exact crossing point, as described in the next section.

3 Exact splitting at the antimeridian

When geometric continuity matters — for contour lines, coastlines loaded from external sources, or range polygons — you can compute the latitude at which the segment crosses \(\lambda_0 \pm 180°\) and split the segment there, inserting two correctly-terminated endpoints at the boundary. The crossing latitude follows from linear interpolation along the segment:

\[ \lambda_{\text{cross}} = \lambda_0 + 180° \cdot \operatorname{sign}(\Delta\lambda) \] \[ \phi_{\text{cross}} = \phi_1 + (\phi_2 - \phi_1)\,\frac{\lambda_{\text{cross}} - \lambda_1}{\lambda_2 - \lambda_1} \]

where \(\Delta\lambda = \lambda_2 - \lambda_1\) is the longitude gap after normalization, and \(\phi_1, \phi_2\) are the endpoint latitudes.

"""
    split_at_antimeridian(lons, lats; lon_0=0.0)

Split a linestring at every antimeridian crossing, returning a
`Vector` of `(lons, lats)` segment tuples, each free of crossings.
Endpoints of adjacent segments share the exact crossing latitude.
"""
function split_at_antimeridian(lons, lats; lon_0=0.0)
    lons_n   = normalize_lon.(lons, lon_0)
1    segments = Tuple{Vector{Float64}, Vector{Float64}}[]
    seg_lons = [lons_n[1]]
    seg_lats = [lats[1]]
    for i in 1:(length(lons_n) - 1)
        Δλ = lons_n[i+1] - lons_n[i]
2        if abs(Δλ) > 180.0
3            λ_cross = lon_0 + 180.0 * sign(Δλ)
            t       = (λ_cross - lons_n[i]) / Δλ
4            ϕ_cross = lats[i] + t * (lats[i+1] - lats[i])
5            push!(seg_lons, λ_cross)
            push!(seg_lats, ϕ_cross)
            push!(segments, (copy(seg_lons), copy(seg_lats)))
6            seg_lons = [λ_cross - 360.0 * sign(Δλ)]
            seg_lats = [ϕ_cross]
        end
        push!(seg_lons, lons_n[i+1])
        push!(seg_lats, lats[i+1])
    end
7    push!(segments, (seg_lons, seg_lats))
    return segments
end

Accumulate completed segments as a vector of (lons, lats) pairs; each returned segment is free of antimeridian crossings.
2

3
The crossing longitude is at the boundary of the current window: \(\lambda_0 + 180°\) or \(\lambda_0 - 180°\) depending on direction of travel.
4
Linear interpolation along the segment gives the exact crossing latitude \(\phi_\text{cross}\).
5
Close the current segment at the crossing point and store it.
6
Open the new segment on the other side of the boundary: shift by \(\pm360°\) so the continuation is correctly positioned in the normalized window.
7
Append the final (possibly unclosed) segment after the loop.

TipPolygon data and GeometryOps.jl

For polygon geometries — species range polygons, biome boundaries, ocean basins — antimeridian splitting is considerably more involved: a polygon that crosses the boundary must be cut into two closed polygons, each with a shared edge running along the antimeridian. Implementing this correctly requires handling winding order, hole geometries, and coordinate ring closure.

Rather than implementing this manually, use GeometryOps.jl, which provides a fix function with an antimeridian_correction pass that handles both linestring and polygon geometries via GeoInterface.jl-compatible objects:

using GeometryOps, GeoInterface

geom_fixed = GeometryOps.fix(geom; corrections=[GeometryOps.AntimeridianCorrection()])

The corrected geometry can then be passed directly to poly! or lines! on a GeoAxis without further coordinate manipulation.
